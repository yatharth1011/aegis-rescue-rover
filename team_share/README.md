## 1. Problem and scope

In a collapsed building or a smoke-filled room, the first job is to find people and get them out. A.E.G.I.S. is a table-top model of that task. A small rover:

1. **listens** for a distress call and estimates its bearing with two microphones;
2. **searches** the arena systematically with a camera, avoiding obstacles and the table edge;
3. **recognises** the victim (a token) with a neural-network detector, ignoring look-alike decoys;
4. **takes it inside** a covered intake bay and closes the door;
5. **returns** to the start along the shortest path it has already driven safely.

**Course constraints:** budget ₹15,000 (Robu.in preferred), team of 4–6, all CAD native to Autodesk Fusion (no AI-generated images).

The original concept used a Jetson Nano and an on-board RL agent. That was dropped: the Jetson alone costs ₹15–22k, and learning by trial and error on a real robot breaks hardware. Instead, the robot keeps the fast safety reflexes, and a laptop runs the heavy perception and planning over Wi-Fi ("edge" computing). Policies are developed and tested in simulation first.

## 2. System architecture

| Layer | Runs on | Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflexes** | ESP32-S3 (on the rover) | Wheel speed control, odometry, table-edge and obstacle safety, scream bearing (GCC-PHAT), Wi-Fi watchdog that stops the rover if commands stop arriving |
| **Vision** | ESP32-CAM (on the rover) | Camera frames streamed over Wi-Fi |
| **Edge brain** | Laptop | Victim detection (YOLOv8n), scream detection, mapping, coverage search, path planning (A*), rescue sequence, return home |

Safety never depends on the link: the edge sensors, distance sensors and watchdog act on the rover itself.

**Search strategy.** The rover always runs a camera coverage search: it keeps a map of which parts of the table it has seen and drives to the viewpoint that reveals the most unseen area per metre travelled. When it hears a scream, the bearing (accumulated over several headings to resolve front/back) biases that choice toward the sound. Only the camera confirms the victim.

**Main hardware** (full list, prices and links in `../to_submit/ItemList_AEGIS_Thursday_1.xlsx`; total ₹11,018, reserve ₹3,982):

- ESP32-S3 DevKitC-1 and ESP32-CAM (OV2640)
- 4 × N20 gear motors with encoders, 2 × TB6612FNG drivers
- 2 × INMP441 MEMS microphones on a 100 mm baseline
- 3 × VL53L0X distance sensors, MPU-6050 gyro
- 5 × TCRT5000 table-edge sensors (front-left, front-right, rear, both rear corners) and 1 intake sensor
- Servo-driven intake door, 3S 18650 pack, 2 × XL4015 5 V regulators
- Laser-cut acrylic decks and four PLA printed parts

## 4. Mechanical design (Autodesk Fusion)

Both models are generated by `Build_AEGIS_Rover/Build_AEGIS_Rover.py`. Run them from Fusion's Scripts list.

- **`Build_AEGIS_Rover` — printable assembly.** 41 components, 34 user parameters, joints with limits, zero interferences.
  - Covered intake bay: the token is guided in by a funnel fin and held by a servo-driven door.
  - Four-wheel drive on N20 motors in printed hangers; two laser-cut decks; camera mast tilted 30° down.
- **`Build_AEGIS_Rover_Detailed` — illustration model.** Every purchased part at its published size and in its own material, a colour-coded wiring harness, and a wiring view with the upper deck hidden.
- **Engraved placement marks** on both decks show each component's outline and label. Engraving is exported on separate DXF layers from cutting.
- **Exports** (`output*/`): STEP, `.f3d`, laser-cut and engrave DXFs, STL for the printed parts, renders.

`engineering/AEGIS_Design_Calculations.md` (generated by `aegis_design_calcs.py`) covers the mass budget, motor torque including pivot turns, per-rail current with regulator losses, battery C-rate and part strength. Worst-case motor load is 27 % of rated torque; worst-case battery draw is 5.84 A (2.66 C).

## 5. Firmware

`firmware/aegis_s3/` is ESP-IDF v5.3 firmware for the ESP32-S3. Its README has the wiring table and bring-up checklist.

- **Navigation task** (core 1, 100 Hz): encoders, gyro, distance sensors, odometry, safety speed filter, wheel PI control, mission state machine.
- **Audio task** (core 0): I2S capture, band-pass filter, GCC-PHAT bearing.
- Algorithms live in a portable C library (`components/aegis_core`), so the firmware, the unit tests and both simulators run the same code.

**Verified:** 32/32 host unit tests pass (GCC-PHAT delay error under 0.07 samples; the safety filter never lets the rover closer than 10 cm). The firmware builds with ESP-IDF v5.3.2 with zero warnings (354 KB). It has not yet run on hardware.

## 6. Simulation and results

### 6.1 3D simulation (`simulation3d/`)

A full-system test on one Mac, described in `simulation3d/README.md`:

- table, room, obstacles, token and decoys in MuJoCo, with real table edges;
- the rover's camera feeding a YOLOv8n detector trained on rendered images (98.9 % recall, 98.7 % precision);
- room acoustics with synthetic screams (scream detector: 80/80 caught, 0/160 false alarms);
- the firmware's own control and audio code;
- a lossy Wi-Fi link with outages;
- the laptop edge brain.

| Demo (random table layouts) | Success: found, captured, home within 15 cm | Median time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hidden token, no sound (40 runs) | **70 %** | 35 s |
| 2. Scream homing (40 runs) | **72.5 %** | 31 s |
| 1. Bad Wi-Fi, about 20 % packet loss (20 runs) | 70 % | |
| 2. Bad Wi-Fi (20 runs) | 50 % | |

The token was found in 37 of 40 runs in both demos. Across all 120 missions the rover fell off the table once (bad Wi-Fi, scream homing).

**Terrain** (3D rigid-body test): crosses steps up to 4 mm and bumps of about 1 cm, climbs 25° slopes, and gets stuck on sharp steps of 6 mm or more (the bay walls sit 4 mm above the ground). Mk2 is a table and indoor-floor robot.

### 6.2 2D software-in-the-loop simulation (`firmware/aegis_s3/sim/`)

The firmware's decision code, unchanged, in 200 random rooms per scenario:

| Scenario | Success | Median time |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal (rule-based policy) | 76 % | 38 s |
| Harsh room | 52.5 % | 44 s |
| Scream beacon off | 37.5 % | 83 s |
| Q-table policy | 22 % | 49 s |

Acoustic homing doubles the success rate and more than halves the mission time.

### 6.3 Design decisions driven by simulation

- **Rule-based navigation over the Q-table.** The early Q-table was trained on a single map and visited only 23 of 48 states; in new rooms it scored 22 % against 76 % for the rule-based policy. The firmware defaults to the rule-based policy.
- **Table-edge falls.** The first design fell in 2 of 40 scream runs. Two rear-corner edge sensors, a 20 cm planning margin from the edge, and a reflex that forbids turning for 1 s after an edge event reduced this to 1 in 120 missions. The cost was hidden-token success falling from 80 % to 70 %.
- **Five firmware bugs found and fixed:** an unreachable docking threshold, jaw collisions, front/back confusion of the two-microphone array, zig-zag search from fixed 90° turns, and no recovery when pinned against an obstacle.
- **Laptop instead of Raspberry Pi or Jetson.** The laptop trains the detector and policies and runs the edge brain during the demo; safety stays on the ESP32. An on-board computer only makes sense for a laptop-free field version and would exceed the budget reserve and power budget.

### 6.4 Known limitations

- The detector is trained on rendered images only; it must be retrained on photos of the real token.
- Screams are synthetic; real recordings are needed.
- Light contact with obstacles is common, and low decoys get nudged because the distance sensors cannot see them.
- Without the start-pad marker, return-home accuracy degrades to 10–40 cm.
